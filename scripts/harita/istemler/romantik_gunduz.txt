The result is a tall vertical (1:3) bird's-eye storybook map of one art-historical period: the 19th century from Romanticism to Realism, a journey from a misty German forest through London and a Pre-Raphaelite stream to Paris and up to the fields of Barbizon.

The picture has five zones from bottom to top, each about one fifth of its height, with one plaza in the middle of each: a misty Romantic forest (bottom), London on the Thames, a Pre-Raphaelite stream, Paris of the Opéra, and the village of Barbizon (top). Paint the whole picture edge to edge as one continuous landscape. Nothing of the flat sketch may remain: no blank paper background, no flat beige strip, no vignettes or framed scenes. The zones flow into each other.

The road is a real painted road whose surface changes with each zone (a mossy forest track, a London cobbled street, a grassy path, smooth Parisian paving, a dusty country lane), but its position and width never change. Where it crosses the Thames it becomes a stone bridge. It runs without any break from the bottom edge (entering at the bottom centre) to the top edge (leaving at the top centre); it never fades out, ends, climbs into the sky or disappears. There is no sea anywhere.

Each of the {{N}} ovals becomes a small, round, empty paved plaza, flat and at ground level like the road (not a raised disc, step or pedestal). Nothing stands on a plaza: no fountain, basin, water, animals, objects or people (a small statue will be placed there later). The period structure named for that level stands right beside its plaza.

The style changes from bottom to top, as the century does:
- Bottom (Romanticism 1): Caspar David Friedrich's sublime stillness: cool mist, pale silvery light, dark oaks, distant blue mountains.
- Second (Romanticism 2): J. M. W. Turner's luminous, swirling atmosphere: golden and orange light dissolving into haze over the river.
- Middle (Pre-Raphaelites): jewel-bright, crisp, minutely detailed nature: emerald greens, every leaf and flower sharp and glowing.
- Fourth (Academic art): the polished, smooth and refined finish of the Paris Salon: creamy stone, gilding, soft even daylight.
- Top (Barbizon and Realism): the earthy plein-air manner of Millet, Corot and Rousseau: warm ochres, browns and soft greens, wide fields under a hazy evening sky.
The styles blend softly where the zones meet; no hard borders.

Use a luminous and lively palette with varied, clear colours; the picture must not look dark, varnished or uniformly brown, and must not be neon. The world is made of famous paintings merged into one land. Each hidden detail is a small scene painted directly into the landscape, never a framed picture, panel, canvas or rectangle pasted on the map, and appears only once. Keep each hidden scene small (no wider than about a third of the picture). At least half of the picture is quiet open space (sky, mist, meadows, water, fields, paved squares). No crowds, no extra scenes, no extra buildings beyond those listed. All figures are fully clothed.

From bottom to top:
1 (lowest plaza): a misty Romantic oak forest. Beside the plaza, the ruin of a Gothic abbey with a tall broken arched window, among bare oaks, as in Friedrich's paintings. Hidden nearby, on a rocky crag above a sea of mist: a man in a dark green coat seen from behind, leaning on a walking stick, looking out over the fog and distant peaks (Caspar David Friedrich, Wanderer above the Sea of Fog).
2: London on the Thames. Beside the plaza, the Gothic Revival Palace of Westminster with its pointed towers and the clock tower, on the river bank. Hidden nearby, further along the river: an older building ablaze at dusk, golden-orange flames and smoke glowing and reflected in the water, tiny dark onlookers on the far bank, painted in a hazy swirl of light (J. M. W. Turner, The Burning of the Houses of Lords and Commons).
3 (middle plaza): a green Pre-Raphaelite stream. Beside the plaza, a small Gothic Revival stone chapel among willows. Hidden nearby, in the stream under a willow: a young woman in a long embroidered silver dress floating on her back among scattered flowers, her hands open, dense glowing reeds and wild roses on the banks (John Everett Millais, Ophelia).
4: Paris. Beside the plaza, the Paris Opéra (Palais Garnier) with its ornate arched façade, gilded statues on the roof and green dome, on a wide boulevard lined with cream-coloured buildings. No hidden painting in this zone.
5 (top plaza): the village of Barbizon. Beside the plaza, a few low stone farmhouses with tiled roofs at the edge of the Fontainebleau forest. Hidden nearby, in a harvested field: three peasant women in coarse skirts and headscarves bending to glean ears of wheat, great haystacks and a cart far behind them (Jean-François Millet, The Gleaners).

The top edge fades into a hazy pale evening sky and the bottom edge into misty forest so the map can join the neighbouring periods; the road reaches both edges. No text, letters, numbers or watermark. No frames or borders. No modern objects. Small figures may appear inside the hidden scenes, but no large characters stand on the road.
